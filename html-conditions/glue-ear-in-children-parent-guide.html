Glue Ear in Children

Understanding middle-ear fluid, hearing changes, and the support children may need.

Parent GuidePending clinician review

Draft review status

Prepared for clinical review on 9 September 2026. This draft is not included in the public resource index.

What is glue ear?

Glue ear, also called otitis media with effusion, is fluid behind the eardrum without the acute signs of an ear infection. It is common in young children and often improves naturally, but it can reduce hearing while the fluid is present.

Possible clues

  • Not responding consistently, asking for repetition, or turning up the television.
  • Speech, language, learning, behaviour, or listening difficulties.
  • Clumsiness, balance concerns, ear discomfort, or a blocked-ear feeling.

These signs have several possible causes. Glue ear cannot be confirmed by symptoms alone.

Assessment and follow-up

A clinician may examine the ears and arrange age-appropriate hearing assessment and tympanometry. The plan depends on whether one or both ears are affected, the degree of hearing difficulty, its impact on daily life, and factors such as Down syndrome or cleft palate. Monitoring may be appropriate for some children; others need audiology or ENT review.

Helping communication

  • Get the child's attention before speaking, face them, and reduce background noise.
  • Speak clearly at a normal pace and check that instructions were understood.
  • Tell nursery or school about the hearing difficulty and arrange supportive seating.
  • Continue follow-up if hearing, speech, learning, or behaviour remains affected.

Treatment discussions

Options may include observation with hearing support, autoinflation for suitable children who can manage it, hearing devices, or grommet surgery. Decisions should be shared with the family and relevant specialists. Antibiotics, antihistamines, decongestants, and oral or nasal corticosteroids are not routine treatments for the fluid itself.

When to seek help sooner

Seek prompt assessment for significant pain, fever or discharge from the ear, sudden or rapidly worsening hearing loss, dizziness, swelling behind the ear, facial weakness, or a child who appears seriously unwell.
